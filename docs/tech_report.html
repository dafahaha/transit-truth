Behavioral Fingerprinting of Large Language Models:
A Statistical Approach to Same-Family API Verification

Abstract

We collect 1,300 responses per model (50 samples × 26 probes, temperature 1.0) for gpt-4o-mini and gpt-4o through one OpenAI-compatible relay. Trivial "random generation" prompts elicit highly stereotyped distributions: gpt-4o-mini returns "7" for a 1–10 choice 100% of the time, "4" on a die roll 96%, and "Cerulean" for a color 74%. After normalizing variant responses (e.g. "Heads." vs "Heads"), 5 of 8 behavioral probes separate the two endpoints at p<0.05 in a chi-square homogeneity test; the animal probe reaches total variation distance (TVD) 0.90 (Okapi 76% for gpt-4o vs. Dolphin 16% for gpt-4o-mini), while tokenizer probes are identical. A simple "response names Okapi" rule gives resubstitution accuracy 88% (95% Wilson CI [0.80, 0.93]). The combined pipeline returns an in-distribution resubstitution posterior of 0.913 (95% credible interval [0.885, 0.937]). We do not claim held-out or provider-ground-truth performance.

1. Introduction

LLMs are increasingly consumed through opaque serving chains where the client cannot confirm that the model answering is the model being paid for. Because a larger model is billed at a higher rate, a relay has a direct incentive to substitute a smaller model while advertising a flagship one. Existing fingerprinting methods rely on long generations, log-probabilities, or crafted prompts and are largely evaluated on cross-family identification. We ask whether trivial "random generation" prompts elicit model-specific behavioral distributions that (i) discriminate same-family siblings where tokenizer fingerprints cannot, and (ii) can be verified at near-zero cost.

2. Related Work

LLM fingerprinting and provenance. Single-token fingerprinting builds an empirical distribution over answers to trivial one-word prompts across 165 models, recovering lineage at a 7.3% EER, but focuses on cross-family identification. RAFP constructs non-invasive black-box fingerprints from rare-region prompts that remain stable under fine-tuning, LoRA, and quantization. Model provenance testing uses multiple-hypothesis testing to decide whether one model derives from another. Model equality testing frames "is this the claimed API model?" as a two-sample problem. We target the complementary fine-grained same-family setting.

Auditing and behavioral biases. Cai et al. audit model substitution; Zhu et al. use a rank-based uniformity test; Sam et al. predict black-box performance from follow-up queries; AgentProv audits agentic providers via tool-use policy probes; CoIn addresses inflated hidden reasoning-token counts. LLMs show documented biases in random number generation and multi-choice selection. We treat these biases as identifying biometric signals and quantify their discriminative value within a model family.

3. Data Collection

For each nominal endpoint we collect 50 responses per probe (1,300 responses) through the OpenAI-compatible relay at temperature 1.0, shuffling probe order, randomizing inter-request intervals (0.3–1.5 s), and rotating User-Agent headers. Wall time is about 15 minutes per model at a cost below $0.01.

Response normalization. Raw responses may carry punctuation or case variants ("Heads." vs "Heads", "heads" vs "Heads"). Before any statistic we strip trailing punctuation and lowercase keys. This matters: the coin-flip probe is not significant after normalization (TVD 0.08, p=0.092), whereas an unnormalized table that splits "Heads" and "Heads." would spuriously report it as significant.

5. Statistical Framework

Chi-square goodness-of-fit against a theoretical uniform reference over J categories:

χ²GoF = Σj=1..J (Oj − Ej)² / Ej,    Ej = n / J

Two-model homogeneity uses the Pearson chi-square on the joint 2×J contingency table (no continuity correction), and total variation distance:

TVD = ½ Σj=1..J |pj − qj|

TVD is a distributional distance, not a classification accuracy. Bayesian updating combines probe-level evidence:

P(H=1 | E) = P(E | H=1)·P(H=1) / P(E), where P(E) = P(E|H=1)P(H=1) + P(E|H=0)P(H=0)

with prior P(H=1)=0.7 and a beta-approximated 95% credible interval. For the Okapi rule we report the Wilson score interval:

( p̂ + z²/2n ± z·√( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n² ) ) / ( 1 + z²/n ),   z=1.96

6.6 End-to-end resubstitution

Scoring a baseline against its own reference: posterior 0.913 (95% credible interval [0.885, 0.937]), KS p=1.000, chi-square p=1.000, aggregate likelihood ratio 4.5. This verifies internal self-consistency; it does not estimate real-world detection accuracy. On easy/medium capability probes both endpoints pass; the bat-and-ball reflection test is answered correctly by gpt-4o-mini but incorrectly by gpt-4o. Latency on this relay is confounded by routing and is not used as an identity signal.

7. Why Do These Fingerprints Exist?

The biases likely arise from training-data frequencies (e.g. cultural salience of "seven"), RLHF alignment that amplifies human-preferred "defaults", and decoder artifacts that skew logits even at temperature 1.0. The striking "Okapi" concentration in gpt-4o suggests a strongly learned default that differs from the smaller sibling, which is why the signal carries identity information orthogonal to the tokenizer.

8. Limitations and Threats to Validity

  1. Collection channel. Both baselines came from one relay, not official endpoints; we claim only that the two nominal endpoints differed on that relay.
  2. Baseline honesty. A baseline inherits whatever model the relay served; an official gold-standard baseline is future work.
  3. Resubstitution. The 0.913 posterior and 88% accuracy are in-sample; held-out accuracy is unmeasured.
  4. Coverage. Only two siblings on one relay; more models/relays needed.
  5. Version drift. Snapshots may shift fingerprints; baselines must be versioned.
  6. Smart routing. A relay can serve the genuine model for probes and a cheaper one for real traffic.
  7. Decoder/normalization. Temperature and output normalization ("Heads." vs "Heads") change distributions.
